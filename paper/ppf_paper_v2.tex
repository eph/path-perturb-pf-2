\begin{abstract}
We study likelihood evaluation for nonlinear DSGE models when the equilibrium transition is available only through finite-horizon path solves rather than a closed-form policy function.
The proposed approximation, pathwise perturbation particle filtering (PPPF), constructs at each filter step a local mixture transition by solving a finite-horizon stacked equilibrium system, linearizing the one-step law conditional on a low-dimensional anticipated-shock proxy, and integrating that proxy with positive-weight quadrature.
The resulting Gaussian-mixture kernel is then evaluated with a Rao--Blackwellized particle filter that samples only the discrete mixture index and propagates the continuous state analytically.

The paper distinguishes four approximation layers: finite-horizon closure, expectation approximation, local linearization, and localization or regime handling.
That decomposition makes clear what is exact, what is approximate, and where bias enters.
In a smooth Burnside asset-pricing example, low-order positive-weight quadrature reproduces the analytic benchmark almost exactly once the continuation object is held fixed.
In a smooth nonlinear filtering benchmark built from the same model, the quadrature-based approximation yields likelihoods that are essentially indistinguishable from a dense-grid benchmark, whereas certainty equivalence performs materially worse.
We then extend the same architecture to a New Keynesian model with an effective lower bound.
In that kinked environment, clustered anchors and a discrete current-shock mixture remove the most obvious impact-response pathology generated by a single-Gaussian innovation closure, but sizable long-sample likelihood gaps remain.
The evidence therefore supports a narrower conclusion: the core PPPF idea is credible for smooth nonlinear likelihood evaluation, while the main unresolved difficulty is transition approximation near occasionally binding constraints.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} nonlinear DSGE estimation; particle filter; Rao--Blackwellization; extended path; stochastic extended path; dynamic perturbation; sigma points; occasionally binding constraints; effective lower bound.\\

\section{Introduction}

Likelihood-based estimation of nonlinear DSGE models is hard for two related reasons.
First, the transition law is rarely available in closed form: it is defined only implicitly by equilibrium conditions with conditional expectations, and those conditions may be approximated only numerically.
Second, once that transition is embedded inside a particle filter, the likelihood estimator can have high Monte Carlo variance precisely in the regions where the model is most nonlinear or where occasionally binding constraints create kinks \citep{KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015,AndrieuDoucetHolenstein2010}.

The standard response is to compute a global or semi-global approximation to the policy functions and then filter the resulting state-space system.
That strategy is often effective, but it is also demanding in medium-scale DSGEs and particularly delicate near occasionally binding constraints.
The alternative studied here is local in state space but sequential in time.
At each date, we solve a finite-horizon perfect-foresight problem along a path relevant for the current filtering distribution, linearize the one-step mapping around that path, and integrate a low-dimensional proxy for future uncertainty by numerical quadrature.
The output is not a global decision rule.
It is a one-step approximate transition kernel constructed for likelihood evaluation.

This perspective is close in spirit to extended path \citep{FairTaylor1983}, stochastic extended path \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025}, and dynamic perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025}, but it serves a different purpose.
Extended path and its stochastic variants are solution methods.
Dynamic perturbation constructs local expansions along realized trajectories.
PPPF embeds those ingredients inside a filtering recursion and therefore has to answer additional questions that solution methods can leave implicit: which objects are random and which are numerical integration nodes, when a quadrature rule may be interpreted as a probability mixture, how regime selection at a kink enters the likelihood recursion, and where approximation bias enters relative to Monte Carlo variance.

The main contribution of the paper is to separate those objects cleanly.
The true DSGE transition kernel is denoted by $K_\theta$.
The finite-horizon stacked solve induces a second kernel that is already approximate because of horizon truncation and the terminal condition.
Linearizing that solve around a reference path induces a third kernel, conditional on an auxiliary anticipated-shock variable and, when needed, on a localization or regime index.
Integrating the auxiliary variable with respect to its Gaussian reference measure produces a Gaussian-mixture kernel.
Only after those approximations are fixed do we evaluate the likelihood with a Rao--Blackwellized particle filter (RBPF).
This decomposition matters because improvement in one layer need not reduce the dominant error in another.

Two applications organize the evidence.
The first is smooth.
In the Burnside asset-pricing recursion, the exact continuation value is available analytically, which makes it possible to isolate the expectation-approximation layer and then embed the same nonlinear object inside a full filtering problem.
Those exercises show that the quadrature layer can be highly accurate in a genuinely nonlinear but smooth environment and that certainty equivalence can fail even there.
The second application is deliberately harder.
In the New Keynesian model with an effective lower bound, the same architecture must also confront regime selection and continuation error at a kink.
There the benchmark shows that Rao--Blackwellization lowers Monte Carlo variance conditional on the approximate transition, while clustered anchors and a discrete current-shock mixture remove identifiable sources of ELB bias without eliminating the remaining long-sample likelihood gap.
The econometric lesson is therefore not that PPPF already solves the kinked case, but that the remaining error can now be concentrated in a much narrower and more interpretable part of the transition approximation.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:lit}

Likelihood-based estimation of DSGE models with nonlinear or non-Gaussian state dynamics typically relies on sequential Monte Carlo methods, often embedded inside parameter-estimation schemes \citep{KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015,AndrieuDoucetHolenstein2010}.
In macroeconomic applications, particle-filter likelihoods have been used for estimation and model comparison in nonlinear DSGEs; see, e.g., \citet{FernandezVillaverdeRubioRamirez2007} and the textbook treatment in \citet{HerbstSchorfheide2016}.
Variance reduction and robustness near sharp nonlinearities have motivated methods such as tempered particle filtering \citep{HerbstSchorfheide2019} and auxiliary/proposal-based particle filters \citep{PittShephard1999}.

Occasionally binding constraint models introduce non-differentiabilities that complicate both solution and filtering.
OccBin \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} and related finite-horizon regime-iteration methods produce piecewise-linear decision rules conditional on a binding pattern over a horizon, and have been widely used for ELB applications \citep{GustHerbstLopezSalidoSmith2017}.
\citet{AruobaCubaBordaHigaFloresSchorfheideVillalvazo2021} develop piecewise-linear continuous (PLC) approximations for OBC DSGEs and a conditionally optimal particle filter (COPF) that exploits the resulting structure to reduce likelihood variance.
PPPF is complementary rather than a replacement.
It retains the filtering perspective of those papers but constructs the transition from pathwise solves and local Jacobians rather than from a global piecewise-linear policy map.

The extended-path method of \citet{FairTaylor1983} solves a finite-horizon perfect-foresight system with a terminal condition, producing a time-varying local approximation to equilibrium dynamics that is often computationally attractive in medium-to-large models.
Stochastic extended path \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025} improves on certainty equivalence by integrating over future shocks to better approximate conditional expectations.
Dynamic perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025} computes first-order expansions along simulated equilibrium paths and emphasizes accuracy along realized trajectories rather than at a fixed steady state.
PPPF combines those ideas with Gaussian-sum filtering: conditional on a low-dimensional shock-path proxy and a regime index, one gets a locally affine state transition that can be propagated analytically inside an RBPF.

Gaussian-sum representations of nonlinear filtering problems date back to \citet{AlspachSorenson1972}.
Rao--Blackwellized particle filters \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000} exploit conditional linear-Gaussian structure to integrate out continuous components analytically via Kalman filtering, reducing Monte Carlo variance relative to a full bootstrap filter \citep{GordonSalmondSmith1993}.
Sigma-point quadrature and related deterministic integration rules are widely used to approximate Gaussian expectations and underlie unscented filtering \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
PPPF uses such quadrature not as a single-Gaussian moment-matching device, but to produce an explicit mixture kernel that can be handled either by branching or by sampling mixture indices within an RBPF.

\section{Nonlinear state-space model}
\label{sec:ssm}

\subsection{Generic state-space formulation}

Let $z_t\in\R^{n_z}$ denote the latent state used by the filter and let $y_t\in\R^{n_y}$ denote the data.
The true transition kernel is $K_\theta(z_t,\dd z_{t+1})$, where $\theta$ collects model parameters.
To avoid symbol collisions later in the New Keynesian application, we reserve $x_t$ for the output gap and use $z_t$ for the generic latent state throughout the methodological sections.

The measurement density is written generically as
\begin{equation}
y_t \mid z_t \sim g_\theta(\cdot \mid z_t).
\label{eq:meas_generic}
\end{equation}
The baseline filtering derivations use the linear-Gaussian special case
\begin{equation}
y_t = M z_t + \eta_t,\qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,R),
\label{eq:meas}
\end{equation}
because that is the setting in which the continuous state can be marginalized exactly by Kalman filtering.
In the NK benchmark below, inflation and the output gap are measured linearly, while the observed policy rate is a lower-censored transformation of the latent state; that measurement is handled by a moment-projected Gaussian update and is therefore kept separate from the four core transition approximations.

\subsection{Equilibrium transition and Gaussian shocks}

The transition is induced by equilibrium conditions and structural innovations.
For exposition we write the one-step law as
\begin{equation}
z_{t+1} = F(z_t,\varepsilon_{t+1};\theta),\qquad \varepsilon_{t+1}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),
\label{eq:true_transition}
\end{equation}
even though in DSGEs the map $F$ is generally not available in closed form.
The paper treats $K_\theta(z,\cdot)$ as the exact object of interest and regards PPPF as a sequence of controlled approximations to that kernel.

\section{From stacked equilibrium conditions to a conditional linear-Gaussian law}
\label{sec:cond_lg}

This section derives the transition used by the filter.
The key distinction is between random shocks and numerical integration devices.
The realized innovation $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ is random and moves the state from $t$ to $t+1$.
The anticipated-shock proxy $\omega_t$ is a numerical device used to approximate expectations beyond $t+1$.
In the ELB benchmark there is a third object in ELB-risk states: a positive-weight node for the realized current innovation.
That node is also a numerical integration device.
It approximates the law of $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ locally near the kink and is not an additional latent shock.

\subsection{A finite-horizon ``stochastic perfect foresight'' system}

Fix a horizon $H\ge 2$.
Let
\[
\omega_t = (\varepsilon_{t+2}^a,\varepsilon_{t+3}^a,\ldots,\varepsilon_{t+H-1}^a)\in \R^{d},
\qquad d=\max\{0,H-2\}n_\varepsilon,
\]
collect a finite horizon of future innovations beyond the realized one-step shock.
The law of $\omega_t$ is induced by the model's structural shocks,
\[
\omega_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Omega),\qquad \Omega=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma,\ldots,\Sigma),
\]
with $H-2$ blocks of $\Sigma$ when $H>2$.
The variable $\omega_t$ is not an extra shock realization on top of $\varepsilon_{t+1}$.
It is a numerical integration variable with respect to $\mu_\Omega$ and is introduced only to approximate the conditional expectations inside the finite-horizon stacked system.

Let $U_t=(z_{t+1},\ldots,z_{t+H-1})$ denote the unknown future path beyond the pinned initial state.
For each admissible localization index $s_t\in\mathcal{S}_t$, define a stacked residual map
\begin{equation}
\Psi_H(U_t; z_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t,s_t,\theta)=0,
\label{eq:stacked_system}
\end{equation}
where $s_t$ may represent a partition cell, a binding pattern, or another device that selects a smooth branch of the equilibrium system.
Equation \eqref{eq:stacked_system} includes equilibrium conditions from $t$ through $t+H-2$ and a terminal condition at $t+H-1$.
When $s_t$ is fixed and the regime is smooth, \eqref{eq:stacked_system} is an ordinary finite-horizon implicit system of the kind solved by extended-path methods.
We denote a solution by $\bar U_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t,s_t;\theta)$ and its first element by $\bar z_{t+1}(z_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t,s_t;\theta)$.

\subsection{Local perturbations around the path}

The stacked system may be solved at a shared anchor, at a small bank of clustered anchors, or at each particle's current state.
With a shared anchor, one uses a single reference state such as the weighted mean of the filtering distribution.
With clustered anchors, one partitions the previous-period particle cloud into a small number of deterministic groups and solves at anchor states $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}$ attached to those groups.
Per-particle anchors set $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}=z_t$.
Moving from shared to clustered to per-particle anchors reduces extrapolation error but increases the number of stacked solves.

Fix an anchor $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}$, a future-shock proxy $\omega_t$, and a regime index $s_t$, and let $\bar U_t$ solve \eqref{eq:stacked_system}.
Linearizing the stacked residual map around that solution and differentiating with respect to the realized one-step innovation $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ yields
\begin{equation}
\tilde z_{t+1}
=
A_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s_t)\,\tilde z_t
+
B_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s_t)\,\varepsilon_{t+1}
+
r_t^{\mathrm{lin}},
\label{eq:lin_dev}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde z_t=z_t-z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}$ and $r_t^{\mathrm{lin}}$ is the local Taylor remainder.
Appendix~\ref{app:ift} derives the Jacobian formulas by the implicit function theorem.
Away from kinks, dropping the remainder and integrating the current innovation under its Gaussian law defines the PPPF one-step approximation conditional on $(\omega_t,s_t,a_t)$:
\begin{equation}
z_{t+1}\mid (z_t,\omega_t,s_t,a_t)
\approx
\mathcal{N}\!\left(m_t^{(a_t,s_t)}(z_t,\omega_t),\,Q_t^{(a_t,s_t)}(\omega_t)\right),
\label{eq:cond_gauss}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
m_t^{(a,s)}(z_t,\omega_t)
&=
\bar z_{t+1}(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s)
+
A_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s)\,(z_t-z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}), \label{eq:cond_mean}\\
Q_t^{(a,s)}(\omega_t)
&=
B_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s)\,\Sigma\,
B_t(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega_t,s)^\top. \label{eq:cond_cov}
\end{align}
If the solve is performed per particle, then $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}=z_t$ and the affine correction term disappears.
If shared or clustered anchors are used, \eqref{eq:cond_mean} is a first-order extrapolation away from the relevant anchor.

Near the ELB kink, the benchmark specification does not always collapse the realized current innovation into the single covariance matrix \eqref{eq:cond_cov}.
Instead, for anchors that are locally at risk of switching between slack and binding regimes, the law of the realized current innovation is approximated by a positive-weight cubature rule $\{(\varepsilon_{t+1}^{(q)},\varpi_q)\}_{q=1}^{Q_t}$.
Conditional on $(a_t,s_t,\omega_t,q_t)$, the method then linearizes around the path associated with the fixed current-shock node $\varepsilon_{t+1}^{(q_t)}$ and obtains a component mean $m_t^{(a,s,q)}(z_t,\omega_t)$ together with a residual covariance $Q_t^{(a,s,q),\mathrm{res}}$ that reflects only regularization or remaining smooth uncertainty.
This current-shock mixture is the relevant refinement near the kink because a single Gaussian closure of $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ can average across different ELB regimes and generate spurious impact responses.

\begin{remark}[Relation to dynamic perturbation and SEP]
Dynamic perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025} also computes first-order expansions along equilibrium paths.
SEP \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025} integrates over future shocks to better approximate conditional expectations than certainty equivalence.
PPPF combines those ideas with a filtering recursion: the path solve and Jacobian calculation are solution steps, while the mixture interpretation and the RBPF are likelihood-evaluation steps.
\end{remark}

\section{Integrating out anticipated shocks}
\label{sec:mixture}

\subsection{Gaussian-mixture transition kernel}

For a fixed anchor group $a_t=a$ and a fixed localization rule or regime weight $\alpha_t^{(a)}(s\mid \omega)$, integrating out $\omega_t$ with respect to $\mu_\Omega$ yields the approximate transition kernel
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf},a}(z_t,\dd z_{t+1})
=
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_t}
\int
\alpha_t^{(a)}(s\mid \omega)\,
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\dd z_{t+1};\,m_t^{(a,s)}(z_t,\omega),Q_t^{(a,s)}(\omega)\right)\,
\mu_\Omega(\dd\omega).
\label{eq:mixture_kernel}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} is a Gaussian-sum kernel \citep{AlspachSorenson1972}.
If localization is deterministic, then $\alpha_t$ is degenerate at one $s$.
If regime uncertainty is carried explicitly, then $\alpha_t$ is a nonnegative mixing rule over regimes.
In either case the integral is with respect to the Gaussian measure of the anticipated-shock proxy, not with respect to the realized shock $\varepsilon_{t+1}$.

In ELB-risk states, the benchmark specification replaces \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} by a mixed discrete-continuous version,
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf},a}(z_t,\dd z_{t+1})
=
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_t}\int \sum_{q=1}^{Q_t}
\alpha_t^{(a)}(s\mid \omega,q)\,\varpi_q\,
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\dd z_{t+1};\,m_t^{(a,s,q)}(z_t,\omega),Q_t^{(a,s,q),\mathrm{res}}(\omega)\right)
\mu_\Omega(\dd\omega),
\label{eq:mixture_kernel_kink}
\end{equation}
where the sum over $q$ approximates the law of the realized current innovation near the kink.
Equation \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel_kink} still defines a nonnegative probability mixture when $\varpi_q\ge 0$ and the regime weights are nonnegative.

We approximate the integral numerically by a $K$-node rule:
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_{\theta,H,K}^{\mathrm{pppf},a}(z_t,\dd z_{t+1})
=
\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_t}\sum_{q=1}^{Q_t}
\tilde w_t^{(a,s,k,q)}
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\dd z_{t+1};\,m_t^{(a,s,q)}(z_t,\omega^{(k)}),Q_t^{(a,s,q),\mathrm{res}}(\omega^{(k)})\right),
\label{eq:quadrature_kernel}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde w_t^{(a,s,k,q)}\ge 0$ and $\sum_{s,k,q}\tilde w_t^{(a,s,k,q)}=1$ when the nodes are used as a probability mixture.
In smooth regions the index $q$ is degenerate and \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel} reduces to the Gaussian-closure case.
When the quadrature rule has signed weights, \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel} is still a valid deterministic quadrature formula but not a sampling distribution.

\subsection{Sigma points as deterministic ``anticipated shock'' draws}

For $\omega\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)\in\R^d$, the basic unscented transform uses $2d+1$ sigma points and associated weights that match mean and covariance and are exact for polynomials up to degree three (for symmetric choices) \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
Sigma points are attractive only when the anticipated-shock proxy is genuinely low dimensional.
If $d$ is large, positive-weight quadrature becomes too expensive for mixture filtering and the claimed variance reduction from Rao--Blackwellization is overwhelmed by component proliferation.

\section{Discrete localization and ``globalization'' of the approximation}
\label{sec:localization}

Quadrature refinement controls only the numerical integration error in \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} and \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel_kink}.
It does not repair bias from horizon truncation, terminal conditions, anchor reuse, or linearization across a kink.
Those errors require separate choices about anchor localization, regime handling, and, near the ELB, the closure used for the realized current innovation.

\subsection{Localization index: partition cells or regime/binding patterns}

Two distinct localization problems matter.
The first concerns anchors.
If a single shared anchor is used, the local affine correction may be transported too far across the filtering distribution.
Clustered anchors provide a middle ground: they partition the particle cloud into a small number of deterministic groups and attach one path solve and Jacobian block to each group.
Per-particle anchors eliminate anchor-reuse error but can be prohibitively expensive.

The second problem concerns the regime or binding pattern of an occasionally binding constraint.
This is the economically relevant case for ELB models.
The correct object to differentiate is not the max operator itself but the smooth equilibrium system conditional on a regime.
OccBin \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} implements a hard regime choice.
PPPF can instead carry a small bind/slack mixture near the boundary.
Even that is not enough when the realized current innovation itself straddles the kink, which is why the benchmark introduces the discrete current-shock mixture in ELB-risk states.

Table~\ref{tab:approx_taxonomy} summarizes the four core approximation axes.
The right comparison is always between neighboring layers in the construction, not between a crude PPPF likelihood and the true DSGE likelihood as if only one approximation had been made.

\section{Filtering and likelihood evaluation}
\label{sec:filtering}

\subsection{PPPF-mixture-RBPF recursion}

Conditional on an anchor group $a_t$, the approximate transition can be written as a discrete mixture of affine-Gaussian components:
\[
z_{t+1}\mid (z_t,a_t,j_t=j)
\approx
A_t^{(j)} z_t + a_t^{(j)} + \xi_{t+1}^{(j)},
\qquad
\xi_{t+1}^{(j)}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q_t^{(j)}).
\]
With linear-Gaussian measurements the continuous state can be marginalized exactly conditional on the discrete history, yielding an RBPF \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000}.
With the censored policy-rate measurement used in the NK benchmark, the same recursion applies but the measurement update is only moment projected, not exact.

Each particle therefore stores a Gaussian state summary $(\mu_t^{(i)},P_t^{(i)})$ and a weight $W_t^{(i)}$.
Let $\ell_t^{(i)}(j)$ denote the predictive density of $y_t$ under particle $i$ and component $j$, after the Gaussian prediction step and before any resampling.
The PPPF recursion is as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
\item Initialize $N$ particles with $(\mu_0^{(i)},P_0^{(i)},W_0^{(i)})$, where $W_0^{(i)}=1/N$.
\item For each date $t=1,\ldots,T$, construct anchor groups.
In the shared-anchor version there is one group and
\[
z_t^{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_{i=1}^N W_{t-1}^{(i)}\mu_{t-1}^{(i)}.
\]
In the clustered-anchor version there are groups $a=1,\ldots,A_t$ with anchors $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a}$ constructed from deterministic summaries of the previous-period particle cloud.
In the per-particle version, each particle forms its own singleton group with anchor $z_t^{\mathrm{ref},i}=\mu_{t-1}^{(i)}$.
\item For each required anchor group and each admissible index $j=(s,k,q)$, solve the stacked system \eqref{eq:stacked_system} at $(z_t^{\mathrm{ref},a},\omega^{(k)},s)$ and compute the corresponding component parameters.
In smooth regions $q$ is degenerate and the component covariance is the Gaussian closure \eqref{eq:cond_cov}.
In ELB-risk regions, build a positive-weight discrete mixture over current-shock nodes and attach the resulting fixed-current-shock local components to the same anchor.
Form the nonnegative anchor-specific mixture weights $\tilde w_t^{(a,j)}$.
If signed quadrature is used, this step cannot feed a sampling-based RBPF and must instead be implemented by deterministic branching.
\item For each particle $i$, assign its deterministic anchor group $a(i)$ and, for each candidate component $j$ in that group's mixture, run the Gaussian predict step
\[
(\mu_{t|t-1}^{(i,j)},P_{t|t-1}^{(i,j)})
=
\big(A_t^{(j)}\mu_{t-1}^{(i)}+a_t^{(j)},\,
A_t^{(j)}P_{t-1}^{(i)}A_t^{(j)\top}+Q_t^{(j)}\big),
\]
and evaluate the predictive density
\[
\ell_t^{(i)}(j)=p(y_t\mid y_{1:t-1},j,\mu_{t-1}^{(i)},P_{t-1}^{(i)}).
\]
\item Choose an index proposal $q_t^{(i)}(j)$.
Two useful choices are the prior mixture $q_t^{(i)}(j)=\tilde w_t^{(a(i),j)}$ and the auxiliary proposal
\[
q_t^{(i)}(j)\propto \tilde w_t^{(a(i),j)}\,\ell_t^{(i)}(j),
\]
which conditions on the current observation.
\item Sample $j_t^{(i)}\sim q_t^{(i)}(\cdot)$ and update the particle weight by
\begin{equation}
W_t^{(i)}
\propto
W_{t-1}^{(i)}\,
\ell_t^{(i)}(j_t^{(i)})\,
\frac{\tilde w_t^{(a(i),j_t^{(i)})}}{q_t^{(i)}(j_t^{(i)})}.
\label{eq:rbpf_weight}
\end{equation}
Under the auxiliary proposal $q_t^{(i)}(j)\propto \tilde w_t^{(a(i),j)}\ell_t^{(i)}(j)$, the correction factor in \eqref{eq:rbpf_weight} collapses to the normalizing constant $\sum_j \tilde w_t^{(a(i),j)}\ell_t^{(i)}(j)$.
\item Conditional on the sampled component, carry out the measurement update and obtain $(\mu_t^{(i)},P_t^{(i)})$.
\item Normalize the weights, update the log-likelihood estimate by the log normalizing constant, compute ESS, and resample if ESS falls below a chosen threshold.
\end{enumerate}

The computational bottleneck is the number of component evaluations per date, not the Kalman algebra itself.
That is why the dimension of $\omega_t$, the number of regimes carried near a kink, and the use of shared versus per-particle anchors are first-order design choices rather than incidental numerical details.

\subsection{Mixture handling and quadrature choices}

Mixture handling is the point at which many otherwise careful papers become ambiguous.
Given a transition of the form $\sum_j \tilde w_t^{(j)}\mathcal{N}(m_t^{(j)},Q_t^{(j)})$, moment-matching the mixture to a single Gaussian generally changes the likelihood.
The PPPF object is the mixture itself.
It must therefore be handled either by exhaustive branching or by sampling the discrete component and accounting for the proposal in \eqref{eq:rbpf_weight}.

This distinction is especially important when the quadrature rule comes from sigma points.
Unscented-transform formulas are often presented as deterministic quadrature rules for moment calculation and may use negative weights.
Such rules are admissible for deterministic integration but inadmissible for a sampling interpretation.
If the paper speaks of ``sampling quadrature nodes,'' then the corresponding weights must be nonnegative.
The benchmark calculations enforce this restriction explicitly.

\section{Approximation theory: kernel, filter, and likelihood}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Quadrature refinement converges to the mixture kernel}

Let $\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf}}$ be the mixture kernel in \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} and let $\widehat K_{\theta,H,K}^{\mathrm{pppf}}$ be its quadrature approximation in \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel}.

\begin{assumption}[Quadrature consistency]
The quadrature rules $(\omega^{(k)},w_k)_{k=1}^K$ are consistent for the Gaussian measure $\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)$:
\[
\sum_{k=1}^K w_k h(\omega^{(k)}) \to \int h(\omega)\,\phi(\omega;0,\Omega)\,\dd\omega
\quad \text{for all bounded continuous } h.
\]
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}[Quadrature refinement]
Assume that, for each fixed $z$ and each fixed anchor group, the component means, component covariances, and nonnegative mixture weights entering \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel} are continuous functions of the numerical integration variables and are dominated by an integrable envelope.
Then, for every bounded continuous test function $\psi$,
\[
\int \psi(z')\,\widehat K_{\theta,H,K}^{\mathrm{pppf}}(z,\dd z')
\to
\int \psi(z')\,\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf}}(z,\dd z').
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This proposition is deliberately modest.
It says only that numerical integration converges to the PPPF mixture kernel defined by the chosen horizon, localization rule, and linearization.
It does not imply convergence to the true DSGE kernel $K_\theta$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Kernel error decomposition}

The approximation taxonomy implies the one-step decomposition
\begin{align}
\|K_\theta(z,\cdot)-\widehat K_{\theta,H,K,J}^{\mathrm{pppf}}(z,\cdot)\|
\;&\le\;
\underbrace{\|K_\theta-\widetilde K_{\theta,H}\|}_{\text{finite horizon and terminal condition}}
+\underbrace{\|\widetilde K_{\theta,H}-K_{\theta,H}^{\omega}\|}_{\text{anticipated-shock proxy}}
+\underbrace{\|K_{\theta,H}^{\omega}-\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf}}\|}_{\text{local linearization}}
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+\underbrace{\|\widehat K_{\theta,H}^{\mathrm{pppf}}-\widehat K_{\theta,H,K}^{\mathrm{pppf}}\|}_{\text{quadrature}}
+\underbrace{\|\widehat K_{\theta,H,K}^{\mathrm{pppf}}-\widehat K_{\theta,H,K,J}^{\mathrm{pppf}}\|}_{\text{localization or regime handling}}.
\label{eq:kernel_decomp}
\end{align}
Here $\widetilde K_{\theta,H}$ denotes the kernel induced by the exact $H$-period stacked problem, $K_{\theta,H}^{\omega}$ is the same kernel after replacing the full future uncertainty with the low-dimensional proxy $\omega_t$, and $\widehat K_{\theta,H,K,J}^{\mathrm{pppf}}$ is the final computable kernel.
Equation \eqref{eq:kernel_decomp} is the bookkeeping device that the benchmark will use to interpret the ablations.

\subsection{From kernel error to filter and likelihood error}

Let $\delta_t=\sup_{z}\|K_\theta(z,\cdot)-\widehat K_{\theta,H,K,J}^{\mathrm{pppf}}(z,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$ denote the one-step kernel error.
Under standard hidden-Markov stability conditions, perturbation bounds imply that filtering and log-likelihood errors inherit the same structure up to forgetting factors; see \citet{DelMoral2004,Whiteley2013,KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015}.

\begin{assumption}[Uniform mixing and bounded likelihood]
There exists $\epsilon\in(0,1)$ and a probability measure $\nu$ on $\mathcal{X}$ such that for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and measurable $A$,
\[
K_\theta(x,A)\ge \epsilon \nu(A),
\]
and the measurement density satisfies $0< g_{\min}\le g_\theta(y\mid x)\le g_{\max}<\infty$ for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and realized $y_t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Strength of the global minorization condition]
Assumption~2 is a standard sufficient condition in stability theory, but it is too strong for many DSGEs.
It is included only to justify the qualitative message that reducing the dominant one-step error in \eqref{eq:kernel_decomp} is the relevant route to improving the likelihood.
The benchmark evidence below should therefore be read as diagnostic rather than as a proof of asymptotic superiority.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Standard perturbation implication]
Under Assumption~2, there exist constants $C<\infty$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$ such that
\[
\|\pi_{\theta,t}-\widehat \pi_{\theta,t}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\le
C \sum_{s=1}^{t} \rho^{t-s}\, \delta_s,
\qquad t\ge 1.
\]
An analogous bound holds for the cumulative log-likelihood error with $\sum_{t=1}^T \delta_t$ on the right-hand side, up to the same forgetting factors.
\end{proposition}

\section{A Compute-Budget Comparison Principle}
\label{sec:dominance}

Let $\ell_\theta$ denote the true log-likelihood and let $\widehat \ell_\theta^{(A)}$ be a particle-based estimator using approximation strategy $A\in\{\mathrm{glob},\mathrm{pppf}\}$.
Under a compute budget $C$, the feasible particle count is roughly $N_A \approx C/c_A$, where $c_A$ is cost per particle per period.
A standard decomposition is
\[
\E\left[(\widehat \ell_\theta^{(A)}-\ell_\theta)^2\right]
=
\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{Bias}_A\big)^2}_{\text{approximation bias}}
+
\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_A^2}{N_A}}_{\text{Monte Carlo variance}}.
\]
Rao--Blackwellization reduces $\sigma_{\mathrm{pppf}}^2$ by integrating out part of the latent state analytically \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000}.
A sufficient dominance condition is therefore
\[
(\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{pppf}})^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{pppf}}^2 \frac{c_{\mathrm{pppf}}}{C}
<
(\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{glob}})^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{glob}}^2 \frac{c_{\mathrm{glob}}}{C}.
\]
This inequality is not a theorem about PPPF in general.
It is only a comparison principle: under a fixed compute budget, lower Monte Carlo variance can offset some transition-side bias, but only if the remaining bias is small enough.
The empirical sections use this decomposition to separate the smooth nonlinear case, where that requirement is plausible, from the kinked ELB case, where transition error remains the binding concern.

\section{Burnside: Smooth Nonlinear Expectations and Filtering}
\label{sec:toy}

The New Keynesian ELB application in Section~\ref{sec:nk_benchmark} combines all four approximation axes.
The present example is deliberately smoother.
It isolates the core nonlinear-expectation problem in an environment with a genuinely curved conditional expectation but without kinks, regime switching, or anchor-localization issues.

Consider the standard Burnside-style asset-pricing recursion
\begin{equation}
v(x_t)=\beta \E_t\!\left[\exp\!\big((1-\gamma)x_{t+1}\big)\bigl(1+v(x_{t+1})\bigr)\right],
\label{eq:burnside_recursion}
\end{equation}
where the state follows the Gaussian AR(1)
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1}=(1-\rho)\mu+\rho x_t+\varepsilon_{t+1},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
\label{eq:burnside_state}
\end{equation}
The nonlinearity comes from the exponential pricing term in \eqref{eq:burnside_recursion}, so Jensen effects matter even though the state dynamics are Gaussian and smooth.

This example admits an analytic benchmark.
Iterating \eqref{eq:burnside_recursion} forward gives
\begin{equation}
v(x)=\sum_{j\ge 1}\beta^j \E\!\left[\exp\!\left((1-\gamma)\sum_{s=1}^j x_{t+s}\right)\middle| x_t=x\right].
\label{eq:burnside_series}
\end{equation}
Because the conditional sum of future $x_{t+s}$ is Gaussian under \eqref{eq:burnside_state}, each term in \eqref{eq:burnside_series} is available in closed form from log-normal moments.
The exact benchmark in this section is therefore an analytic infinite-series object rather than a separate numerical global solution.

The certainty-equivalence approximation drops the conditional variance terms implicit in \eqref{eq:burnside_series}.
The quadrature approximation instead integrates the one-step shock $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ with a positive-weight sigma-point rule while keeping the continuation value exact.
This first exercise is not yet a full PPPF likelihood problem.
There is no filtering recursion and no discrete regime index.
Its role is to show how much of the smooth nonlinear error comes from replacing a curved Gaussian expectation by either a degenerate one-node rule or a nondegenerate low-order quadrature rule, holding the continuation object fixed.

\subsection{Smooth nonlinear filtering benchmark}

Before turning to the ELB application, we consider a smooth nonlinear filtering problem built from the same Burnside state process.
The latent state still follows \eqref{eq:burnside_state}, while the observation equation is
\begin{equation}
y_t=v(x_t)+\eta_t,
\qquad
\eta_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\eta^2).
\label{eq:burnside_measurement}
\end{equation}
This design is useful because the transition is smooth, the state is one-dimensional, and the only substantive approximation issue is how the nonlinear expectation embedded in $v(x)$ is represented.
A dense-grid filter using the exact value function therefore provides a high-accuracy benchmark, while dense-grid filters based on certainty equivalence and one-step sigma-point quadrature isolate the expectation-approximation component without the additional complications created by kinks, regime switching, or anchor reuse.

\section{Extension: a New Keynesian model with an ELB}
\label{sec:nk_benchmark}

This section specifies the benchmark environment used for the numerical comparison.
The benchmark is intentionally narrow: it is designed to diagnose where PPPF bias enters in a simple ELB environment, not to establish dominance over every alternative solution method.

\subsection{Core equilibrium conditions}

We consider a canonical three-equation New Keynesian model with an effective lower bound (ELB) on the nominal rate.
Let $x_t$ denote the output gap, $\pi_t$ inflation, and $i_t$ the nominal interest rate.
A standard log-linearized NK system is
\begin{align}
x_t &= \E_t x_{t+1} - \sigma^{-1}\Big(i_t - \E_t \pi_{t+1} - r_t^n\Big), \label{eq:nk_is}\\
\pi_t &= \beta \E_t \pi_{t+1} + \kappa x_t, \label{eq:nk_pc}\\
i_t^\star &= \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_x x_t + \nu_t + i^{\mathrm{ss}}, \label{eq:taylor}\\
i_t &= \max\{ \underline i,\; i_t^\star\}. \label{eq:elb}
\end{align}
The exogenous processes are Gaussian AR(1) laws for $r_t^n$ and $\nu_t$.
The ELB condition \eqref{eq:elb} creates the kink that drives the regime problem.

\subsection{State-space representation and measurement}

A minimal latent state is
\[
z_t=(x_t,\pi_t,r_t^n,\nu_t)^\top.
\]
The policy rate is not stored as an independent state variable; it is an algebraic function of $z_t$ through \eqref{eq:taylor}--\eqref{eq:elb}.
The observable vector is
\[
y_t=(\pi_t^{\mathrm{obs}},x_t^{\mathrm{obs}},i_t^{\mathrm{obs}})^\top.
\]
Inflation and the output gap are measured linearly with Gaussian noise.
The policy rate is measured as a lower-censored function of $z_t$ with Gaussian observation error.
Consequently, the transition-side RBPF is exact conditional on the discrete index only for the first two measurements; the third measurement is handled by a Gaussian moment projection in the benchmark calculations.

\subsection{Competing methods}

The benchmark compares three likelihood-evaluation strategies that differ in how they approximate the transition kernel near the ELB kink and how they control Monte Carlo variance.
First, an OccBin-style piecewise-linear extended-path solution \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} is embedded in a bootstrap particle filter.
Second, the benchmark also considers a PLC-inspired baseline that interpolates the global discretized benchmark and filters only the exogenous state with an observation-informed Gaussian proposal.
This is best interpreted as a PLC/COPF-style comparator, not as a line-by-line reimplementation of \citet{AruobaCubaBordaHigaFloresSchorfheideVillalvazo2021}.
Third, PPPF constructs an anchor-specific Gaussian-mixture transition by integrating over a short-horizon anticipated-shock proxy and, in ELB-risk states, by replacing the single-Gaussian closure of the realized current innovation with a small positive-weight discrete mixture.

\subsection{Proposed evaluation metrics}

Because particle filters yield stochastic likelihood approximations, the comparison emphasizes runtime per likelihood evaluation, Monte Carlo variability of repeated log-likelihood estimates on fixed data, ESS profiles (and resampling frequency), and impulse responses relative to the discretized global benchmark.
The aim is to distinguish approximation bias from Monte Carlo variance.
Mean log-likelihood gaps and IRF discrepancies are interpreted as kernel differences.
Dispersion across repeated runs and ESS are interpreted as Monte Carlo diagnostics.

\subsection{Experimental design}

The empirical design has two applications.
The first is the Burnside asset-pricing model in \eqref{eq:burnside_recursion}--\eqref{eq:burnside_measurement}.
It is intentionally smooth and yields two distinct diagnostics: an analytic expectation benchmark and a full nonlinear filtering benchmark.
The second is a New Keynesian benchmark with an effective lower bound.
That extension combines horizon truncation, expectation approximation, local linearization, and regime handling at a genuine kink.

In the NK--ELB experiment, the OccBin extended-path horizon is $H=20$.
We solve the discretized global benchmark (Tauchen grid) and simulate a dataset of length $T=250$ from that benchmark, adding small independent measurement noise on $(\pi_t,x_t,i_t)$.
For likelihood evaluation we use $N=256$ particles and repeat each filter run $N_{\mathrm{rep}}=30$ times with different random seeds to measure Monte Carlo variability.

To attribute PPPF likelihood bias in Table~\ref{tab:nk_summary} to specific approximation steps, the ablations vary the quadrature resolution $K$ (including $K=1$ certainty equivalence), the horizon and terminal condition, the degree of anchor reuse, and the regime treatment at the ELB boundary.
The direct IRF diagnostic in Figure~\ref{fig:nk_irf} separately evaluates the one-step current-shock closure used near ELB-risk states.
Reporting the mean log-likelihood gap to the discretized benchmark under these perturbations turns the bias--variance tradeoff into an explicit bias attribution exercise.

The discretized global benchmark induces a finite-state hidden Markov model for the exogenous state.
Let $g_t\in\{1,\ldots,S\}$ index the Tauchen grid point for $(r_t^n,\nu_t)$ and let $P\in\R^{S\times S}$ be the transition matrix.
Solving the discretized model gives an expectation-consistent observable map $h(g)=(\pi(g),x(g),i(g))^\top$.
With Gaussian measurement noise, the likelihood of the discretized benchmark is obtained exactly by the HMM forward recursion
\[
\alpha_{t|t-1}(g')=\sum_{g=1}^S \alpha_{t-1}(g)\,P(g,g'),\qquad
\tilde \alpha_t(g)=\alpha_{t|t-1}(g)\,p(y_t\mid g),\qquad
z_t=\sum_{g=1}^S \tilde \alpha_t(g),
\]
with $\alpha_t(g)=\tilde \alpha_t(g)/z_t$ and $\log p(y_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T \log z_t$.
This likelihood is exact for the discretized benchmark, not for the underlying continuous-state DSGE.
That distinction is essential: the benchmark is a high-quality comparison object, not literal truth.

\subsection{Illustrative results}

We begin with the smooth Burnside application, for which the exact continuation value is available from the analytic series \eqref{eq:burnside_series}.
Figure~\ref{fig:burnside_irf} reports the response of the continuation value to a one-standard-deviation shock to $x_0$ under the exact benchmark, certainty equivalence, and a one-step positive-weight sigma-point quadrature approximation with exact continuation.
The certainty-equivalence curve misses most of the nonlinear amplification created by the exponential pricing kernel.
By contrast, the quadrature curve is almost indistinguishable from the analytic benchmark in this scalar Gaussian environment.
That result should be interpreted narrowly.
It does not show that low-order quadrature is universally accurate.
It shows that, once the continuation object is held fixed, the Jensen effect in this smooth one-dimensional expectation can be captured almost exactly by a small positive-weight node set.

\section{Conclusion}

PPPF should be understood as a structured approximation scheme for likelihood evaluation, not as a single approximation.
Its output depends on a horizon choice, a proxy for future uncertainty, a local-linearization rule, and a localization or regime device.
Once those layers are stated explicitly, the role of the RBPF becomes clear: it reduces Monte Carlo variance conditional on the approximate transition, but it does not remove transition-side bias.

The smooth Burnside application shows that the core idea is viable in the environment for which it is conceptually best suited.
Low-dimensional positive-weight quadrature captures the relevant Jensen effect almost exactly in the analytic continuation problem, and the same approximation yields likelihoods in the smooth nonlinear filtering benchmark that are essentially indistinguishable from a dense-grid benchmark.
That evidence matters because it separates generic nonlinearity from the harder problems created by kinks.

The ELB extension shows both the promise and the limitation of pushing the same architecture to occasionally binding constraints.
Rao--Blackwellization raises ESS sharply, clustered anchors improve likelihood relative to a single shared anchor, and an ELB-aware discrete current-shock mixture removes one identifiable impact-response pathology created by a single-Gaussian closure of the realized innovation.
But those gains do not eliminate the main quantitative problem: in the long-sample NK benchmark PPPF remains far from the discretized global likelihood.
The most credible path forward is therefore additional work on continuation and regime handling near the ELB rather than a claim that PPPF is already ready for structural estimation in kinked DSGEs.

\section{Deriving the conditional linear system via the implicit function theorem}
\label{app:ift}

This appendix derives \eqref{eq:lin_dev} from the stacked system.
Let
\[
U_t=(z_{t+1},z_{t+2},\ldots,z_{t+H-1})\in\R^{(H-1)n_z}.
\]
Write the regime-conditioned stacked system as
\[
\Psi_H(U_t;z_t,\omega_t,s_t,\theta)=0.
\]
Suppose $\bar U_t$ solves this system for fixed $(z_t,\omega_t,s_t)$ and assume that the Jacobian with respect to $U_t$ is nonsingular:
\[
D_{U}\Psi_H(\bar U_t;z_t,\omega_t,s_t,\theta)\ \text{is invertible}.
\]
Then the implicit function theorem yields a local map $U_t=G(z_t,\omega_t,s_t)$ with derivatives
\[
D_{z}G = -\big(D_U\Psi_H\big)^{-1} D_z \Psi_H,
\qquad
D_{\omega}G = -\big(D_U\Psi_H\big)^{-1} D_\omega \Psi_H.
\]
To incorporate the realized innovation $\varepsilon_{t+1}$, augment the period-$t+1$ equations by the contemporaneous shock terms and differentiate with respect to $\varepsilon_{t+1}$.
The first block row of $D_zG$ gives $A_t$ and the first block row of $D_\varepsilon G$ gives $B_t$.
Because the stacked Jacobian is sparse and banded, these derivatives are obtained by solving sparse linear systems rather than by differentiating a global policy map.

\section{Unscented-transform sigma points}
\label{app:ut}

For $\omega\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)\in\R^d$, the basic sigma-point set uses $2d+1$ points:
\[
\omega^{(0)}=0,\qquad
\omega^{(i)}=+\left[\sqrt{(d+\lambda)\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},\qquad
\omega^{(i+d)}=-\left[\sqrt{(d+\lambda)\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},
\quad i=1,\ldots,d,
\]
with weights
\[
w_0^m=\frac{\lambda}{d+\lambda},\qquad w_i^m=\frac{1}{2(d+\lambda)}\;\;(i\ge 1),
\]
and covariance weights $w_0^c=w_0^m + (1-\alpha^2+\beta)$, $w_i^c=w_i^m$.
Standard choices are $\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\beta=2$ (Gaussian prior), $\kappa=0$ \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
If sigma points are used as a probability mixture, the relevant weights are the mixture weights, not the covariance weights, and they must satisfy $w_k\ge 0$.
For the UT parameterization above, this requires $\lambda\ge 0$.
The common small-$\alpha$ settings used in unscented Kalman filtering typically violate this restriction and should therefore be interpreted as signed quadrature rather than as a sampling distribution.
When nonnegative weights are required, a simple positive-weight rule for $\omega\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)$ uses $2d$ points:
\[
\omega^{(i)}=+\sqrt{d}\,\left[\sqrt{\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},\qquad
\omega^{(i+d)}=-\sqrt{d}\,\left[\sqrt{\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},
\quad i=1,\ldots,d,
\qquad
w_i=\frac{1}{2d}.
\]
This yields a bona fide probability mixture and is exact for Gaussian polynomials up to degree three.
