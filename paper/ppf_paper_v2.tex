\author{Ed \and \emph{(coauthor to be added)}}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We formalize a likelihood-evaluation strategy for nonlinear DSGE models based on \emph{pathwise} (a.k.a.\ extended-path) perfect-foresight solves and first-order linearizations around those paths.
At each filtering step we augment the model with an auxiliary ``anticipated-shock'' object---a finite horizon of future structural innovations---that is used to approximate conditional expectations in the equilibrium conditions.
Conditional on this auxiliary object (and on a discrete \emph{localization index} that encodes the relevant piece of the state space or the binding pattern of constraints), the model is linear-Gaussian: the one-step transition is Gaussian with parameters given by the pathwise Jacobians, and the measurement equation is assumed linear with Gaussian noise.
Integrating out the auxiliary object yields a Gaussian-mixture transition kernel.
This structure enables a Rao--Blackwellized particle filter that marginalizes the high-dimensional continuous state with a Kalman filter and samples only the low-dimensional auxiliary object and discrete localization index.

We derive the conditional linear-Gaussian representation from the stacked equilibrium system via the implicit function theorem, establish conditions for quadrature and localization refinement to converge to a well-defined mixture kernel, and provide stability-based bounds linking one-step kernel error to filtering and log-likelihood error.
We also study a New Keynesian model with an effective lower bound (ELB) on the nominal rate and compare PPPF-based likelihood evaluation to OccBin and to PLC/COPF-style approximations.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} nonlinear DSGE estimation; particle filter; Rao--Blackwellization; extended path; stochastic extended path; dynamic perturbation; sigma points; occasionally binding constraints; effective lower bound.\\
\textbf{JEL:} C11, C15, C61, E32.

\section{Introduction}

Likelihood-based estimation of nonlinear DSGE models is difficult because equilibrium dynamics are nonlinear, typically available only implicitly via a system of equilibrium conditions with conditional expectations, and frequently feature kinks from occasionally binding constraints (OBCs) such as an effective lower bound (ELB).
A direct likelihood approach nests the resulting nonlinear state-space system inside a particle filter, and for parameter inference typically nests that filter inside an MCMC/SMC scheme \citep{KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015,AndrieuDoucetHolenstein2010}.
Two difficulties dominate: constructing a transition law that is accurate enough for likelihood-based inference, and controlling Monte Carlo variance of the likelihood estimator when the latent state is high-dimensional or the model has sharp nonlinearities.

A widely used strategy is to compute a global (or semi-global) approximation to the policy function (projection methods, value-function iteration, Smolyak/sparse grids, endogenous grid methods, discrete regime grids, etc.) and then filter the resulting nonlinear state-space system.
This can be accurate, but it can be computationally costly in medium-to-large DSGEs and becomes particularly challenging near kinks where standard bootstrap particle filters can suffer severe weight degeneracy.

This paper formalizes an alternative approximation strategy geared specifically toward likelihood evaluation.
Instead of approximating the policy function globally, we approximate the \emph{local} dynamics in the neighborhood of \emph{paths} that are relevant under the data.
At each filtering step we (i) solve for a finite-horizon equilibrium path using a perfect-foresight/extended-path system conditional on a low-dimensional auxiliary object that represents a finite horizon of ``anticipated'' future shocks, and then (ii) compute a first-order expansion around this path.
This is in the spirit of the Extended Path (EP) method of \citet{FairTaylor1983} and its stochastic generalizations \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025}, and is closely related to Dynamic Perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025}, which computes first-order expansions along simulated equilibrium paths.

The central novelty is to embed this pathwise linearization inside a \emph{filter}.
Conditional on the auxiliary ``anticipated-shock'' object (and, when needed, on a discrete localization index encoding a regime/binding pattern or a partition cell in state space), the one-step transition is approximately linear-Gaussian, with parameters obtained from Jacobians of the stacked equilibrium system.
Integrating out the auxiliary object by sigma points or other quadrature yields a Gaussian-mixture (Gaussian-sum) transition kernel \citep{AlspachSorenson1972}.
With linear-Gaussian measurement equations, this structure supports a Rao--Blackwellized particle filter (RBPF) that marginalizes a large continuous state block with a Kalman filter and samples only the low-dimensional auxiliary variables and discrete localization indices \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000}.

The framework exposes a bias--variance--cost tradeoff.
Finite-horizon solves, expectation approximations, and local linearization introduce approximation bias, while Rao--Blackwellization can substantially reduce Monte Carlo variance relative to a bootstrap particle filter \citep{GordonSalmondSmith1993}.
Whether PPPF improves on global-solution-based likelihood evaluation depends on compute budgets, the effective dimension of the latent state, and how often the data drive the economy into regions where nonlinearities matter.

\subsection{Contributions and organization}

We derive a conditional linear-Gaussian approximation to DSGE transitions from stacked equilibrium conditions using the implicit function theorem, which clarifies the connection between EP/SEP, dynamic perturbation, and time-varying local linearizations along equilibrium paths.
We then construct a Gaussian-mixture transition kernel by integrating over an anticipated-shock auxiliary variable using quadrature and explain when this quadrature can be interpreted as a probability mixture inside an RBPF.
The paper also states an RBPF recursion tailored to this mixture structure, including mixture handling (branching versus sampling), general index proposals, and the role of localization indices for globalization and for regime selection in OBC models.
Finally, we formulate an NK--ELB benchmark that permits direct comparison to OccBin and PLC/COPF-style approaches and separates approximation bias from Monte Carlo variability in likelihood evaluation.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This section situates PPPF relative to work on nonlinear DSGE likelihood evaluation, OBC solutions, and mixture/Rao--Blackwellized filtering.

\subsection{Nonlinear DSGE likelihood evaluation and particle filtering}

Likelihood-based estimation of DSGE models with nonlinear or non-Gaussian state dynamics typically relies on sequential Monte Carlo methods, often embedded inside parameter-estimation schemes \citep{KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015,AndrieuDoucetHolenstein2010}.
In macroeconomic applications, particle-filter likelihoods have been used for estimation and model comparison in nonlinear DSGEs; see, e.g., \citet{FernandezVillaverdeRubioRamirez2007} and the textbook treatment in \citet{HerbstSchorfheide2016}.
Variance reduction and robustness near sharp nonlinearities have motivated methods such as tempered particle filtering \citep{HerbstSchorfheide2019} and auxiliary/proposal-based particle filters \citep{PittShephard1999}.

\subsection{Occasionally binding constraints and piecewise approximations}

OBC models introduce non-differentiabilities that complicate both solution and filtering.
OccBin \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} and related finite-horizon regime-iteration methods produce piecewise-linear decision rules conditional on a binding pattern over a horizon, and have been widely used for ELB applications \citep{GustHerbstLopezSalidoSmith2017}.
\citet{AruobaCubaBordaHigaFloresSchorfheideVillalvazo2021} develop piecewise-linear continuous (PLC) approximations for OBC DSGEs and a conditionally optimal particle filter (COPF) that exploits the resulting structure to reduce likelihood variance.
PPPF is complementary: it uses pathwise linearizations and can incorporate an OBC binding pattern as a discrete localization index.

\subsection{Extended-path, stochastic extended-path, and pathwise linearization}

The extended-path method of \citet{FairTaylor1983} solves a finite-horizon perfect-foresight system with a terminal condition, producing a time-varying local approximation to equilibrium dynamics that is often computationally attractive in medium-to-large models.
Stochastic extended path \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025} improves on certainty equivalence by integrating over future shocks to better approximate conditional expectations.
Dynamic perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025} computes first-order expansions along simulated equilibrium paths and emphasizes accuracy along realized trajectories rather than at a fixed steady state.
PPPF can be viewed as combining these ideas with a filtering recursion: build a pathwise linearization (conditional on a finite-dimensional shock-path proxy) and integrate it inside the likelihood evaluator.

\subsection{Gaussian-sum filtering, sigma points, and Rao--Blackwellization}

Gaussian-sum (mixture) representations of nonlinear filtering problems date back to \citet{AlspachSorenson1972}.
Rao--Blackwellized particle filters \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000} exploit conditional linear-Gaussian structure to integrate out continuous components analytically via Kalman filtering, reducing Monte Carlo variance relative to a full bootstrap filter \citep{GordonSalmondSmith1993}.
Sigma-point quadrature and related deterministic integration rules are widely used to approximate Gaussian expectations and underlie unscented filtering \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
PPPF uses such quadrature not as a single-Gaussian moment-matching device, but to produce an explicit mixture kernel that can be handled either by branching or by sampling mixture indices within an RBPF.

\subsection{Latent state and measurement}
Let $(x_t)_{t\ge 0}$ be an $\R^{n_x}$-valued latent state and $(y_t)_{t\ge 1}$ observed data.
We assume a linear measurement equation with Gaussian noise:
\begin{equation}
y_t = M x_t + \eta_t,\qquad \eta_t\sim \mathcal{N}(0,R),
\label{eq:meas}
\end{equation}
where $M$ and $R$ may depend on parameters $\theta$.

\subsection{Equilibrium transition and Gaussian shocks}
The state transition is induced by DSGE equilibrium conditions and structural innovations.
For exposition we write it as a Markov mapping
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = F(x_t,\varepsilon_{t+1};\theta),\qquad \varepsilon_{t+1}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),
\label{eq:true_transition}
\end{equation}
even though in DSGEs $F$ is usually not available in closed form.

Let $K_\theta(x,\cdot)$ denote the true Markov kernel of $x_{t+1}\mid x_t=x$ induced by \eqref{eq:true_transition}.

\section{From stacked equilibrium conditions to a conditional linear-Gaussian law}
\label{sec:cond_lg}

This section derives the object the filter will use: a Gaussian approximation to $K_\theta(x_t,\cdot)$ conditional on an auxiliary object (anticipated shocks) and a discrete localization index.

\subsection{A finite-horizon ``stochastic perfect foresight'' system}

Fix a horizon $H\ge 2$.
Let
\[
\omega_t = (\varepsilon_{t+2}^a,\varepsilon_{t+3}^a,\ldots,\varepsilon_{t+H-1}^a)\in \R^{d},
\qquad d=\max\{0,H-2\}n_\varepsilon,
\]
collect a sequence of \emph{anticipated} shocks \emph{beyond the one-step innovation} over the horizon (so for $H=2$, $\omega_t$ is empty).
We treat $\omega_t$ as an auxiliary integration variable with the Gaussian law induced by the shock process:
\[
\omega_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Omega),\qquad \Omega=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma,\ldots,\Sigma),
\]
where the block diagonal has $H-2$ copies of $\Sigma$ (and is vacuous when $H=2$).
Conceptually, $\omega_t$ is a numerical device used to approximate conditional expectations, not an additional structural shock, and it is \emph{distinct} from the realized one-step innovation $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ that moves the state from $t$ to $t+1$.

Throughout, structural innovations $\varepsilon_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$ are random.
By contrast, $\omega$ is an integration variable with respect to the Gaussian measure $\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)$ that we later approximate numerically by quadrature; it should not be interpreted as an additional shock realization on top of $\varepsilon_{t+1}$.
Operationally, the stacked EP/SEP-style system is solved at a baseline $\varepsilon_{t+1}=0$, while $\omega_t$ perturbs the future-expectations terms over $t+2,\ldots,t+H-1$.

Let $X_{t:t+H-1}=(x_t,x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{t+H-1})$ and define a stacked residual mapping
\[
\mathcal{R}(X_{t:t+H-1};x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t,\theta)=0
\]
encoding equilibrium conditions from $t$ through $t+H-2$ plus a terminal condition at $t+H-1$ (e.g.\ $x_{t+H-1}\approx x^\star$).
This is the finite-horizon stacked system solved in EP/SEP and related perfect-foresight solvers \citep{FairTaylor1983,AdjemianJuillard2025}.
We denote by
\[
\bar X_{t:t+H-1}=\bar X(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t;\theta)
\]
a solution of the stacked system.

\subsection{Local perturbations around the path}

In general the EP/SEP system may be solved (i) \emph{per particle} at $x_t$ or (ii) at a \emph{reference/anchor state} $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ (e.g.\ a weighted mean of particles) to avoid solving per particle.
To keep the derivation aligned with implementable filters, we distinguish these cases explicitly.

Fix an anchor $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ and let $\bar X_{t:t+H-1}=\bar X(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t;\theta)$ denote the stacked solution path.
Define deviations $\tilde x_s = x_s-\bar x_s$ for $s=t,\ldots,t+H-1$, so in particular $\tilde x_t=x_t-x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ because the initial condition in the stacked system is pinned at $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$.
Linearizing $\mathcal{R}$ at $\bar X$ yields a sparse block system linking $\tilde x_{t:t+H-1}$ to \emph{unexpected} innovations.
To keep the derivation transparent, we focus on the one-step mapping from $(x_t,\varepsilon_{t+1})$ to $x_{t+1}$.
Let $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ be the \emph{unexpected} shock realization at $t+1$ (mean zero).
Then the linearized system implies
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_{t+1}
=
A_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)\,\tilde x_t
+
B_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)\,\varepsilon_{t+1},
\label{eq:lin_dev}
\end{equation}
where $A_t,B_t$ are obtained from the Jacobian of the stacked system (details in Appendix~\ref{app:ift}).

Equation \eqref{eq:lin_dev} yields a \emph{conditional} linear-Gaussian transition:
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1}\mid (x_t,\omega_t,x_t^{\mathrm{ref}})
\;\approx\;
\mathcal{N}\!\left(m(x_t,\omega_t),\,Q(x_t,\omega_t)\right),
\label{eq:cond_gauss}
\end{equation}
with
\[
m(x_t,\omega_t)=\bar x_{t+1}(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)+A_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)\,(x_t-x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}),
\qquad
Q(x_t,\omega_t)=B_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)\,\Sigma\,B_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)^\top.
\]
If one solves the stacked system per particle, one sets $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}=x_t$ and the mean reduces to $m(x_t,\omega_t)=\bar x_{t+1}(x_t,\omega_t)$.
If instead one uses a shared anchor $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ (to amortize stacked solves across particles or to propagate a Gaussian belief with mean $\mu_t$), the affine correction term $A_t(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega_t)(x_t-x_t^{\mathrm{ref}})$ transfers the pathwise linearization away from the anchor and constitutes an additional approximation.

\begin{remark}[Relation to dynamic perturbation and SEP]
Dynamic perturbation \citep{MennuniRubioRamirezStepanchuk2025} also computes first-order expansions along equilibrium paths.
SEP \citep{AdjemianJuillard2025} integrates over future shocks to better approximate conditional expectations than certainty equivalence.
The PPPF construction can be viewed as: (i) using a SEP-style draw/quadrature node $\omega_t$ to build the reference path, (ii) computing a dynamic-perturbation-style linearization around that path, and (iii) integrating out $\omega_t$ inside a filter.
\end{remark}

\section{Integrating out anticipated shocks}
\label{sec:mixture}

\subsection{Gaussian-mixture transition kernel}

Integrating out $\omega_t$ yields an approximate transition kernel
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_\theta(x_t,\dd x_{t+1})
=
\int
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\dd x_{t+1};\,m(x_t,\omega),Q(x_t,\omega)\right)\,
\phi(\omega;0,\Omega)\,\dd\omega.
\label{eq:mixture_kernel}
\end{equation}
This is a Gaussian mixture (Gaussian-sum) transition \citep{AlspachSorenson1972}.
In the per-particle version one sets $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}=x_t$, so \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} defines an approximate Markov kernel in $x_t$ alone.
In the reference-state shortcut, one holds $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ fixed within the time-$t$ update (e.g.\ the weighted mean of particles) and interprets \eqref{eq:cond_gauss}--\eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} as a controlled local approximation around that anchor.
We approximate the integral by a $K$-node rule (sigma points, Gauss--Hermite, sparse grids, quasi-Monte Carlo, or Monte Carlo):
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_{\theta,K}(x_t,\dd x_{t+1})
=
\sum_{k=1}^K w_k\,
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\dd x_{t+1};\,m(x_t,\omega^{(k)}),Q(x_t,\omega^{(k)})\right),
\qquad \sum_{k=1}^K w_k=1.
\label{eq:quadrature_kernel}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sigma points as deterministic ``anticipated shock'' draws}

For $\omega\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)\in\R^d$, the basic unscented transform uses $2d+1$ sigma points and associated weights that match mean and covariance and are exact for polynomials up to degree three (for symmetric choices) \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
Sigma points are attractive when $d$ is small (e.g.\ a small number of shocks and short horizon).

\section{Discrete localization and ``globalization'' of the approximation}
\label{sec:localization}

Sigma-point refinement controls the numerical integration error in \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel}, but does \emph{not} by itself eliminate the local-linearization and horizon/terminal-condition errors.
This section introduces a second approximation axis that can yield a genuine global refinement scheme.

\subsection{Localization index: partition cells or regime/binding patterns}

Let $s_t\in\{1,\ldots,J\}$ be a discrete localization index.
Two cases are especially relevant.
In the first, $s_t$ indexes a partition of a compact set $\mathcal{X}\subset\R^{n_x}$ into cells $\{\mathcal{X}_j\}_{j=1}^J$ with anchors $x^{(j)}\in\mathcal{X}_j$.
For each anchor (and each quadrature node $\omega^{(k)}$) one computes $(\bar x_{t+1}^{(j,k)},A^{(j,k)},B^{(j,k)})$ and defines a piecewise approximation by assigning each $x_t$ to its cell and using the corresponding local parameters.
When the continuous state is Rao--Blackwellized (stored as a Gaussian belief rather than as draws), this convention must be made explicit; a common choice is deterministic localization at an anchor/mean $s_t=L(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}})$ or $s_t=L(\mu_{t-1})$, while an alternative is probabilistic localization by cell mass under the Gaussian belief.

In the second, $s_t$ encodes the binding/slack pattern of an occasionally binding constraint over a horizon (a regime path).
OccBin \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} selects such a regime path by iterating on guesses until the constraint is satisfied, while filtering approaches can treat the regime as latent and integrate over it by branching or sampling.
Aruoba et al.\ \citep{AruobaCubaBordaHigaFloresSchorfheideVillalvazo2021} construct globally piecewise-linear and continuous decision rules and derive a conditionally optimal particle filter (COPF) that exploits this structure.
This regime conditioning is also the appropriate way to define pathwise Jacobians at kinks induced by max/min constraints: conditional on a regime the equilibrium system is smooth, Jacobians are well defined, and the filter can integrate over regimes via the discrete index.

\subsection{A simple approximation theorem for localization refinement}

To formalize ``convergence to global'' under partition refinement, we state a generic result.
For simplicity, consider a setting where $(m(x,\omega),Q(x,\omega))$ is smooth in $x$ for each $\omega$ (away from kinks).
Let $\widehat m_J(x,\omega)$ and $\widehat Q_J(x,\omega)$ be piecewise-linear approximations built from anchors with mesh size
\[
\Delta_J = \max_{j\le J} \sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_j}\|x-x^{(j)}\|.
\]

\begin{assumption}[Smoothness in $x$]
For each $\omega$ in the support of the quadrature rule and all $x\in\mathcal{X}$, the maps $x\mapsto m(x,\omega)$ and $x\mapsto Q(x,\omega)$ are twice continuously differentiable with uniformly bounded second derivatives.
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}[Localization refinement]
Under the smoothness assumption, there exists $C<\infty$ such that
\[
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}} \|m(x,\omega)-\widehat m_J(x,\omega)\|\le C \Delta_J^2,
\qquad
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}} \|B(x,\omega)-\widehat B_J(x,\omega)\|\le C \Delta_J^2,
\]
for each fixed $\omega$, provided $\widehat m_J,\widehat B_J$ are constructed by first-order Taylor (or equivalent local linear) approximations on each cell.
Define $\widehat Q_J(x,\omega)=\widehat B_J(x,\omega)\,\Sigma\,\widehat B_J(x,\omega)^\top$, which is positive semidefinite by construction.
Then there exists $C_Q<\infty$ such that
\[
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}} \|Q(x,\omega)-\widehat Q_J(x,\omega)\|\le C_Q \Delta_J^2.
\]
Hence if $\Delta_J\to 0$ as $J\to\infty$, then the approximation errors vanish uniformly while preserving the covariance-matrix constraints needed by the Kalman recursion.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This is the standard ``mesh $\to 0$'' globalization logic: piecewise linearization converges for smooth maps.
For OBCs, the map is typically only piecewise smooth and the rate can degrade near the kink.
In that case one refines the partition more aggressively near the kink set, or treats the kink via an explicit discrete regime as in OccBin and PLC/COPF.
\end{remark}

\section{Filtering and likelihood evaluation}
\label{sec:filtering}

\subsection{Gaussian-sum representation}

Under \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel}, the approximate transition can be written by introducing a discrete index $k_t\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ with $\Pr(k_t=k)=w_k$:
\[
x_{t+1}\mid (x_t,k_t=k) \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k(x_t),Q_k(x_t)).
\]
With localization $s_t$, the index becomes $(s_t,k_t)$.

\subsection{Rao--Blackwellized particle filter}

Because the measurement is linear-Gaussian and the transition is conditionally Gaussian given $(s_t,k_t)$, we can marginalize the continuous state by Kalman filtering conditional on each particle's discrete history.
A Rao--Blackwellized particle filter (RBPF) samples $(s_t,k_t)$ (or, in a continuous version, samples $\omega_t$) and updates the conditional Gaussian state distribution analytically \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000}.

\subsection{PPPF-mixture-RBPF recursion}

This subsection states the filtering recursion in a form suitable for likelihood evaluation.
For ease of exposition we assume the mixture components are locally affine in $x_t$:
\[
 x_{t+1}\mid (x_t, s_t=s, k_t=k)
 \;\approx\;
 A_{t}^{(s,k)} x_t + a_{t}^{(s,k)} + \xi_{t+1}^{(s,k)},
 \qquad
 \xi_{t+1}^{(s,k)}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q_{t}^{(s,k)}),
\]
and the measurement equation is linear-Gaussian as in \eqref{eq:meas}.
Each RBPF particle stores a Kalman-filter state (mean/covariance) for the continuous state conditional on its discrete history.
\smallskip
Let $\ell_t^{(i)}(j)$ denote the predictive likelihood of $y_t$ under particle $i$ and component $j$, obtained from the Kalman predict step under that component.
The recursion can be summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
\item Initialize each particle $i$ with a Gaussian prior $(\mu_0^{(i)},P_0^{(i)})$ and particle weight $W_0^{(i)}=1/N$.
\item For each $t=1,\ldots,T$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
\item Choose an anchor state $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ at which to build pathwise parameters.
Two common choices are (i) per-particle anchors $x_t^{\mathrm{ref},(i)}=\mu_{t-1}^{(i)}$ (more accurate but more costly) and (ii) a shared anchor $x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_i W_{t-1}^{(i)}\mu_{t-1}^{(i)}$ (computationally convenient but introducing anchor-reuse error).
\item Build (or update) a mixture approximation to the one-step transition at the chosen anchor(s), producing component weights $\tilde w_{t}^{(s,k)}$ and parameters $(A_{t}^{(s,k)},a_{t}^{(s,k)},Q_{t}^{(s,k)})$.
In PPPF these parameters come from an EP/SEP-style stacked solve at $(x_t^{\mathrm{ref}},\omega^{(k)},s)$ followed by pathwise Jacobians, with $Q=B\Sigma B^\top$.
\item For each particle $i$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
\item Sample a discrete index $j_t^{(i)}=(s_t^{(i)},k_t^{(i)})$ from a proposal $q_t^{(i)}(\cdot)$.
A baseline choice is the prior $q_t^{(i)}(j)=\tilde w_t^{(j)}$; an auxiliary proposal that conditions on $y_t$ is $q_t^{(i)}(j)\propto \tilde w_t^{(j)}\,\ell_t^{(i)}(j)$.
\item Kalman predict under the sampled component $j_t^{(i)}$: $(\mu_{t|t-1}^{(i)},P_{t|t-1}^{(i)})$.
\item Kalman update given $y_t$, obtaining $(\mu_t^{(i)},P_t^{(i)})$ and the predictive likelihood $\ell_t^{(i)}(j_t^{(i)})$.
\item Update the particle weight by multiplying by the predictive density and the proposal correction factor:
\[
 W_t^{(i)} \propto W_{t-1}^{(i)}\, \ell_t^{(i)}(j_t^{(i)})\,
 \frac{\tilde w_t^{(j_t^{(i)})}}{q_t^{(i)}(j_t^{(i)})}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\item Normalize weights, compute ESS, and resample if ESS falls below a threshold (e.g.\ $0.5N$).
\item Accumulate the log-likelihood estimate by a stable log-sum-exp of the unnormalized particle weights.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The defining feature of the RBPF is that conditional on the discrete index $j_t$ the continuous state remains linear-Gaussian and can be integrated analytically by a Kalman filter, reducing Monte Carlo variance relative to a bootstrap particle filter.
Auxiliary index proposals that condition on $y_t$ can further stabilize the recursion by targeting mixture components with high predictive likelihood \citep{PittShephard1999}.
The dominant computational cost per period is governed by the number of evaluated components $(s,k)$ and the cost of the associated stacked solves and Jacobian evaluations.

\subsection{Mixture handling and quadrature choices}

A central methodological point is \emph{mixture handling}.
Given a mixture transition $\sum_k w_k \mathcal{N}(m_k,Q_k)$, averaging the means $m_k$ and covariances $Q_k$ to obtain a single Gaussian is generally incorrect; it destroys tail behavior and Jensen effects.
Instead, one must treat the mixture either by branching (expanding particles) or by sampling $k$ and accounting for the mixture density in the proposal.

In the RBPF setting, branching corresponds to spawning children for each $(s,k)$ and then resampling.
Sampling corresponds to drawing $(s,k)$ from their prior weights (or an auxiliary proposal) and updating particle weights accordingly.

When quadrature nodes are used as mixture components inside an RBPF, the weights must define a probability distribution.
Sigma-point rules (e.g.\ the unscented transform) are often presented as deterministic quadrature and may assign negative weights under common parameterizations.
Negative weights are acceptable for certain moment computations, but they are incompatible with sampling mixture indices.
Accordingly, probability-mixture implementations require nonnegative-weight rules (e.g.\ positive-weight cubature, Gauss--Hermite with positive weights, or Monte Carlo with equal weights).
Alternatively, the integral over $\omega_t$ can be implemented by branching and aggregating signed contributions without interpreting quadrature weights as sampling probabilities.

For OBC models, a natural localization index is the binding pattern over the EP horizon (as in OccBin), which yields piecewise-linear dynamics conditional on the regime.
More generally, localization can represent a partition of the state space, refined adaptively near kinks, so that first-order Taylor expansions are accurate within each cell.
Section~\ref{sec:localization} formalizes this ``globalization'' logic.

\section{Approximation theory: kernel, filter, and likelihood}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Quadrature refinement converges to the mixture kernel}

Let $\widehat K_\theta$ be the mixture kernel \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel} and $\widehat K_{\theta,K}$ its $K$-node quadrature approximation \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel}.

\begin{assumption}[Quadrature consistency]
The quadrature rules $(\omega^{(k)},w_k)_{k=1}^K$ are consistent for the Gaussian measure $\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)$:
\[
\sum_{k=1}^K w_k h(\omega^{(k)}) \to \int h(\omega)\,\phi(\omega;0,\Omega)\,\dd\omega
\quad \text{for all bounded continuous } h.
\]
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}[Weak convergence of $\widehat K_{\theta,K}\to \widehat K_\theta$]
Assume $(m(x,\omega),Q(x,\omega))$ is continuous in $\omega$ for each fixed $x$ and that dominated convergence applies to the Gaussian density integrand.
Then for each fixed $x$ and each bounded continuous test function $\psi$,
\[
\int \psi(x')\,\widehat K_{\theta,K}(x,\dd x') \to \int \psi(x')\,\widehat K_{\theta}(x,\dd x').
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This convergence holds to the mixture kernel $\widehat K_\theta$, not to the true DSGE kernel $K_\theta$.
Closing the remaining gap requires controlling the local-linearization error, horizon truncation/terminal-condition error, and expectation-approximation error in the underlying stochastic perfect-foresight step.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Kernel error decomposition}

Write the total kernel error as
\begin{align}
\|K_\theta(x,\cdot)-\widehat K_{\theta,K,J}(x,\cdot)\|
\;&\le\;
\underbrace{\|K_\theta-\widetilde K_{\theta,H,p}\|}_{\text{horizon/expectation approximation}}
+\underbrace{\|\widetilde K_{\theta,H,p}-\widehat K_{\theta}\|}_{\text{local linearization}}
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+\underbrace{\|\widehat K_{\theta}-\widehat K_{\theta,K}\|}_{\text{quadrature}}
+\underbrace{\|\widehat K_{\theta,K}-\widehat K_{\theta,K,J}\|}_{\text{localization}}.
\end{align}
where $\widetilde K_{\theta,H,p}$ denotes the kernel induced by the \emph{exact} finite-horizon stochastic perfect-foresight solution with $p$ future periods' expectations approximated by integration over $\omega$ (no linearization), and $\widehat K_{\theta,K,J}$ denotes the computable PPPF kernel with $K$ quadrature nodes and localization mesh index $J$.
In the baseline construction in Section~\ref{sec:cond_lg}, $\omega_t$ covers periods $t+2,\ldots,t+H-1$, so $p=H-2$.

\subsection{From kernel error to filter and likelihood error}

Let $\delta=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}} \|K_\theta(x,\cdot)-\widehat K_{\theta,K,J}(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$ be the worst-case one-step kernel error.
Under standard mixing/forgetting conditions for hidden Markov models, errors in the transition kernel propagate to errors in filtering distributions and predictive likelihoods; see, e.g., \citet{DelMoral2004,Whiteley2013,KantasDoucetSinghMaciejowski2015}.

\begin{assumption}[Uniform mixing and bounded likelihood]
There exists $\epsilon\in(0,1)$ and a probability measure $\nu$ on $\mathcal{X}$ such that for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and measurable $A$,
\[
K_\theta(x,A)\ge \epsilon \nu(A),
\]
and the measurement density satisfies $0< g_{\min}\le g_\theta(y\mid x)\le g_{\max}<\infty$ for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and realized $y_t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Strength of the global minorization condition]
Assumption~3 is a standard sufficient condition used in stability theory, but it is often unrealistically strong for continuous-state DSGEs with near-deterministic transitions and informative measurements (where global minorization can fail).
We include it only to make precise the qualitative message that \emph{one-step transition-kernel errors propagate into filtering and likelihood errors under forgetting}.
In applied DSGE filtering one typically relies on local stability, bounded-moment conditions, and diagnostic checks rather than global minorization.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Filter perturbation bound (standard form)]
Under the mixing and bounded-likelihood assumption, there exist constants $C<\infty$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$ such that the filtering distributions satisfy
\[
\|\pi_{\theta,t}-\widehat \pi_{\theta,t}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\le
C \sum_{s=1}^{t} \rho^{t-s}\, \delta,
\qquad t\ge 1.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Log-likelihood perturbation bound (sketch)]
Under the same conditions, there exists $C_\ell<\infty$ such that
\[
\left| \log p_\theta(y_{1:T}) - \log \widehat p_\theta(y_{1:T}) \right|
\le
C_\ell\, T\, \delta
\]
up to geometric forgetting factors.
\end{corollary}

\section{When can PPPF be ``better'' than a global solution?}
\label{sec:dominance}

Let $\ell_\theta$ denote the \emph{true} log-likelihood and let $\widehat \ell_\theta^{(A)}$ be a particle-based estimator using approximation strategy $A\in\{\mathrm{glob},\mathrm{pppf}\}$.
Under a compute budget $C$, the feasible particle count is roughly $N_A \approx C/c_A$, where $c_A$ is cost per particle per period.

A standard decomposition is
\[
\E\left[(\widehat \ell_\theta^{(A)}-\ell_\theta)^2\right]
=
\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{Bias}_A\big)^2}_{\text{approximation bias}}
+
\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_A^2}{N_A}}_{\text{Monte Carlo variance}}.
\]
Rao--Blackwellization reduces $\sigma_{\mathrm{pppf}}^2$ by integrating out (part of) the latent state analytically \citep{DoucetFreitasMurphyRussell2000}.
A sufficient ``dominance'' condition is therefore:
\[
(\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{pppf}})^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{pppf}}^2 \frac{c_{\mathrm{pppf}}}{C}
<
(\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{glob}})^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{glob}}^2 \frac{c_{\mathrm{glob}}}{C}.
\]
This inequality clarifies how relative approximation bias, Monte Carlo variance, and per-period computational cost jointly determine whether PPPF yields a lower mean squared error than a global-solution-based likelihood evaluator under a fixed compute budget.

\section{A minimal toy example (no DSGE background)}
\label{sec:toy}

The NK--ELB benchmark in Section~\ref{sec:nk_benchmark} is economically meaningful but combines multiple approximation axes (finite horizon, expectation approximation, local linearization, and regime handling at a kink).
This subsection records a minimal toy example whose ``truth'' can be computed to high accuracy and that isolates the \emph{anticipated-shock quadrature} and \emph{mixture-handling} components of PPPF.

Consider the following one-dimensional toy state-space model.
Let $x_t\in\R$ and $y_t\in\R$ satisfy
\[
y_t = x_t + \eta_t,\qquad \eta_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,r),
\]
and define a transition that is conditionally linear-Gaussian given an auxiliary Gaussian variable $\omega_t$:
\[
x_{t+1}\mid (x_t,\omega_t) = \rho x_t + b\,\tanh(\omega_t) + \sigma \varepsilon_{t+1},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),
\qquad
\omega_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega).
\]
Integrating out $\omega_t$ yields a Gaussian mixture kernel of the form \eqref{eq:mixture_kernel}--\eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel}.
This captures in the simplest possible way the PPPF logic: an auxiliary Gaussian object represents expectation-relevant nonlinear terms (here $b\tanh(\omega)$), and quadrature produces an explicit mixture transition rather than a single Gaussian moment match.

This toy model isolates three issues.
Certainty equivalence corresponds to setting $\omega_t\equiv 0$, which yields a single Gaussian transition with mean $\rho x_t$.
Quadrature bias can be studied by comparing $K=1$ to increasing $K$ in \eqref{eq:quadrature_kernel} under a nonnegative-weight rule when mixture indices are sampled.
Finally, mixture handling can be studied by comparing (i) sampling or branching over mixture indices $k_t$ to (ii) collapsing the mixture to a single Gaussian by moment matching, which is generally not likelihood preserving.
Because the model is one-dimensional, a high-accuracy reference likelihood can be computed by fine-grid discretization or a large-$K$ Monte Carlo mixture approximation, which provides a transparent verification of the ``quadrature-as-mixture'' construction.

\section{Benchmark: a New Keynesian model with an ELB}
\label{sec:nk_benchmark}

This section specifies a concrete benchmark that directly connects to the OccBin and PLC/COPF literatures.

\subsection{Core equilibrium conditions}

We consider a canonical three-equation New Keynesian model with an effective lower bound (ELB) on the nominal rate.
Let $\tilde y_t$ denote the output gap, $\pi_t$ inflation, and $i_t$ the nominal interest rate (deviations from steady state, appropriately scaled).
A standard log-linearized NK system is
\begin{align}
\tilde y_t &= \E_t \tilde y_{t+1} - \sigma^{-1}\Big(i_t - \E_t \pi_{t+1} - r_t^n\Big), \label{eq:nk_is}\\
\pi_t &= \beta \E_t \pi_{t+1} + \kappa \tilde y_t, \label{eq:nk_pc}\\
i_t^\star &= \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_{\tilde y}\tilde y_t + \nu_t, \label{eq:taylor}\\
i_t &= \max\{ \underline i,\; i_t^\star\}. \label{eq:elb}
\end{align}
Here $r_t^n$ is a natural-rate (or preference) shock and $\nu_t$ a monetary policy shock; both follow AR(1) laws with Gaussian innovations.
Appendix~\ref{app:nk} provides a brief microfoundation and discusses common variations (habit, indexation, wage rigidities).

The occasionally binding ELB constraint \eqref{eq:elb} introduces a kink and makes the decision rules nonlinear and piecewise smooth.

\subsection{State-space representation and measurement}

A minimal latent state can be
\[
x_t^{\mathrm{state}} = (\tilde y_t,\pi_t,i_t,r_t^n,\nu_t)^\top,
\]
with observables
\[
y_t = (\tilde y_t^{\mathrm{obs}},\pi_t^{\mathrm{obs}},i_t^{\mathrm{obs}})^\top,
\qquad
y_t = M x_t^{\mathrm{state}} + \eta_t.
\]
Because the ELB affects the transition, likelihood evaluation requires a nonlinear filter unless the solution imposes a structure that can be exploited.

\subsection{Competing methods}

The benchmark compares three likelihood-evaluation strategies that differ in how they approximate the transition kernel near the ELB kink and how they control Monte Carlo variance.
First, an OccBin-style piecewise-linear extended-path solution \citep{GuerrieriIacoviello2015} can be embedded in a bootstrap particle filter (or related inversion-filter variants when shocks are approximately identified).
Second, the piecewise-linear continuous (PLC) approximation of \citet{AruobaCubaBordaHigaFloresSchorfheideVillalvazo2021} delivers globally piecewise-linear decision rules and supports a conditionally optimal particle filter (COPF) that exploits the resulting structure; our benchmark includes a PLC-based baseline that filters only the exogenous state and uses a conditionally optimal proposal under a locally affine PLC measurement mapping.
Third, PPPF constructs a Gaussian-mixture transition by integrating over a short-horizon anticipated-shock object $\omega_t$ and, for OBC models, conditions on a discrete localization index $s_t$ that selects a smooth binding or slack system.
In ELB applications this regime conditioning also resolves the differentiability issue induced by the max operator, and motivates carrying a small mixture over regimes near the boundary rather than relying on hard regime selection.

\subsection{Proposed evaluation metrics}

Because particle filters yield stochastic likelihood approximations, the comparison emphasizes runtime per likelihood evaluation, Monte Carlo variability of repeated log-likelihood estimates on fixed data, ESS profiles (and resampling frequency), and impulse responses relative to the discretized global benchmark.
The aim is to distinguish approximation bias (differences in mean likelihood/IRFs induced by different approximate kernels) from Monte Carlo variance (dispersion across repeated filter runs).

\subsection{Experimental design}

The empirical design combines an analytic verification exercise (Burnside recursion) for the expectation approximation component with a New Keynesian ELB benchmark.
The benchmark experiment isolates the interaction between regime nonlinearity, mixture-based transitions, and likelihood evaluation.

In the NK--ELB experiment, the OccBin extended-path horizon is $H=20$.
We solve the discretized global benchmark (Tauchen grid) and simulate a dataset of length $T=250$ from that benchmark, adding small independent measurement noise on $(\pi_t,\tilde y_t,i_t)$.
For likelihood evaluation we use $N=256$ particles and repeat each filter run $N_{\mathrm{rep}}=30$ times with different random seeds to measure Monte Carlo variability.

To attribute PPPF likelihood bias in Table~\ref{tab:nk_summary} to specific approximation steps, one can vary one component at a time.
In particular, the ablations vary the quadrature resolution $K$ (including $K=1$ certainty equivalence), the horizon and terminal condition $(H,T_H)$, the degree of anchor reuse, and the regime treatment at the ELB boundary.
Reporting the mean log-likelihood gap to the discretized benchmark under these perturbations turns the bias--variance tradeoff into an explicit bias attribution exercise.

The discretized global benchmark used in this experiment induces a finite-state hidden Markov model (HMM) for the exogenous state, so its likelihood can be computed exactly by the standard forward recursion.
Let $g_t\in\{1,\ldots,S\}$ index the Tauchen grid point for $(r_t^n,\nu_t)$ and let $P\in\R^{S\times S}$ be the resulting transition matrix.
Solving the discretized global model delivers an expectation-consistent mapping from each grid point to observables $h(g)=(\pi(g),\tilde y(g),i(g))^\top$.
With Gaussian measurement noise, $y_t\mid g_t \sim \mathcal{N}(h(g_t),R)$, the log-likelihood of the discretized benchmark can be computed exactly by the standard forward recursion:
\[
\alpha_{t|t-1}(g')=\sum_{g=1}^S \alpha_{t-1}(g)\,P(g,g'),\qquad
\tilde \alpha_t(g)=\alpha_{t|t-1}(g)\,p(y_t\mid g),\qquad
z_t=\sum_{g=1}^S \tilde \alpha_t(g),
\]
with $\alpha_t(g)=\tilde \alpha_t(g)/z_t$ and $\log p(y_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T \log z_t$.
This likelihood is exact for the \emph{discretized benchmark} (grid + global policy functions), not for the underlying continuous-state DSGE.

\section{Conclusion}

PPPF replaces a global nonlinear approximation of DSGE policy functions with many local linearizations around stochastic perfect-foresight paths, integrating over an anticipated-shock object by sigma points/quadrature and optionally over regimes via a discrete localization index.
With Gaussian shocks and linear measurement equations, this yields a Gaussian-mixture state-space model amenable to Rao--Blackwellized particle filtering.
The paper derives the method from stacked equilibrium conditions, establishes convergence statements for quadrature and localization refinement, and links one-step kernel error to filtering and log-likelihood error using stability arguments.
A New Keynesian ELB benchmark offers a natural comparison to the OccBin and PLC/COPF literatures.

The benchmark likelihood computed by the forward recursion is exact only for the discretized global model used to generate the data, not for the underlying continuous-state DSGE.
Consequently, differences in likelihood across methods reflect both approximation error in the transition kernel and the discretization inherent in the benchmark.
In addition, while the PLC-based baseline in Section~\ref{sec:nk_benchmark} is designed to connect to the PLC/COPF perspective, a fully regime-exact COPF that integrates over PLC partition indices without local linearization remains a natural extension.

The NK--ELB results highlight that PPPF can deliver substantial variance reduction (high ESS, low Monte Carlo variability) while still exhibiting approximation bias when any of the four approximation axes is too crude.
Reducing this bias requires sharpening the regime treatment at the ELB boundary (including regime mixtures near the kink), designing genuinely low-dimensional anticipated-shock objects so that positive-weight quadrature is feasible at moderate $K$, and implementing the ablation suite in Section~\ref{sec:nk_benchmark} to attribute remaining discrepancies to $(H,K)$, anchor reuse, and regime handling.

\section{Deriving the conditional linear system via the implicit function theorem}
\label{app:ift}

This appendix provides a derivation of \eqref{eq:lin_dev} from the stacked residual system.
Let $Y$ denote the stacked unknowns over the horizon excluding the pinned initial state:
\[
Y = (x_{t+1},x_{t+2},\ldots,x_{t+H-1})\in \R^{(H-1)n_x}.
\]
Write the stacked equilibrium conditions as
\[
\mathcal{F}(Y;x_t,\omega_t,\theta)=0.
\]
Suppose $\bar Y$ solves the system for given $(x_t,\omega_t)$.
Assume the Jacobian with respect to $Y$ is nonsingular:
\[
D_Y \mathcal{F}(\bar Y;x_t,\omega_t,\theta)\;\;\text{is invertible}.
\]
Then by the implicit function theorem there exists a local mapping $Y=G(x_t,\omega_t)$ with derivatives
\[
D_{x}G = -\big(D_Y\mathcal{F}\big)^{-1} D_x \mathcal{F},
\qquad
D_{\omega}G = -\big(D_Y\mathcal{F}\big)^{-1} D_\omega \mathcal{F}.
\]
To incorporate an \emph{unexpected} shock $\varepsilon_{t+1}$, one augments the residual function to include the contemporaneous shock in the appropriate period-$t+1$ equations, and differentiates with respect to $\varepsilon_{t+1}$.
Because $D_Y\mathcal{F}$ is sparse block-banded (typically block tridiagonal for one-step-ahead expectations), solving for these derivatives amounts to solving a sparse linear system, which is computationally attractive in large DSGEs.

Extracting the first block row of $D_x G$ gives the mapping from $x_t$ perturbations to $x_{t+1}$ perturbations, yielding $A_t$.
Similarly, the first block row of $D_{\varepsilon} G$ gives $B_t$.
Plugging into $x_{t+1}=\bar x_{t+1}+\tilde x_{t+1}$ gives \eqref{eq:lin_dev}.

\section{Unscented-transform sigma points}
\label{app:ut}

For $\omega\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)\in\R^d$, the basic sigma-point set uses $2d+1$ points:
\[
\omega^{(0)}=0,\qquad
\omega^{(i)}=+\left[\sqrt{(d+\lambda)\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},\qquad
\omega^{(i+d)}=-\left[\sqrt{(d+\lambda)\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},
\quad i=1,\ldots,d,
\]
with weights
\[
w_0^m=\frac{\lambda}{d+\lambda},\qquad w_i^m=\frac{1}{2(d+\lambda)}\;\;(i\ge 1),
\]
and covariance weights $w_0^c=w_0^m + (1-\alpha^2+\beta)$, $w_i^c=w_i^m$.
Standard choices are $\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\beta=2$ (Gaussian prior), $\kappa=0$ \citep{JulierUhlmann2004}.
If sigma points are used \emph{as a probability mixture} (sampling a discrete index $k_t$), the quadrature weights must satisfy $w_k\ge 0$.
For the UT parameterization above, this requires $\lambda\ge 0$ (hence $w_0^m\ge 0$); the common ``small-$\alpha$'' settings used in unscented Kalman filtering typically violate this and should be treated as \emph{signed quadrature} rather than a mixture distribution.
When nonnegative weights are required, a simple positive-weight rule for $\omega\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)$ uses $2d$ points:
\[
\omega^{(i)}=+\sqrt{d}\,\left[\sqrt{\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},\qquad
\omega^{(i+d)}=-\sqrt{d}\,\left[\sqrt{\Omega}\right]_{\cdot i},
\quad i=1,\ldots,d,
\qquad
w_i=\frac{1}{2d}.
\]
This yields a bona fide probability mixture (all weights equal and nonnegative) and is exact for polynomials up to degree three under a Gaussian reference measure.
